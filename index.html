Nirnaya is the Hindi word for a decision. It comes from Sanskrit, from roots that mean “to lead out”: you weigh what’s in front of you and arrive at a conclusion.

That shaped what we built. People make decisions in many languages, and they don’t only type them: they speak, and they point at photos and documents. So every Nirnaya model reads text, images and audio, and treats Indian languages as core work.

What a decision model is

Most decisions don’t need a conversation

Daniel Kahneman split human thinking into two modes. System 1 is fast and automatic: you read a face or hear that a voice is angry before you’ve tried to. System 2 is slow and deliberate: it’s what you use for long division.

Software is full of System 1 work. Which team should get this ticket? Is this message fraud? Should the agent call a tool here? Is this answer backed by the document? Each one is a choice between known options, and a product may make millions of them a day.

A decision model is built for exactly that. You give it a state and typed questions. It returns one answer per question, with a probability on every option, in a single pass. There is no text to generate and no reply to parse.

Why it exists

    
Why a normal LLM isn’t enough

    
An LLM can answer all of these questions. Running it in production as a decision engine is where the trouble starts.

    

      
It writes when you only need a choice
An LLM produces its answer one token at a time, and often explains itself first. You pay for those tokens in time and money. Nirnaya reads the request once and scores every option at once.

      
Its confidence isn’t measured
“I’m 90% sure” is a sentence, and nothing makes it true. A decision model’s probabilities are trained and checked against outcomes, so you can set a threshold on them.

      
Its answers need parsing
Free text can drift, add a preamble or name an option you didn’t offer. Here the response is always one of your options, with a probability for each.

      
Many options get expensive
Nirnaya handles up to 255 options per question and several questions in one request, still in one pass.

The family

    
Same way of working, two choices

    
Flash and Turbo share a training recipe, a request format and an engine. Moving between them means changing the model you load. They differ in useful ways, and neither wins everywhere.

    

      
Nirnaya Flash
Choose it for speed, listening and small footprint. It’s the faster model and the smaller one to run. On audio it’s on par with its larger sibling, ahead on 8 of 11 listening tests, and it’s level with Turbo at picking the right API call (97.4 against 97.2).

      
Nirnaya Turbo
Choose it for images and harder knowledge. It leads on public text and vision benchmarks, by a wide margin on images, and on Hindi general knowledge.

What to measure

    
A good decision model is right, honest and fast

    

      
Probability
every option
The output itself. A 94% answer and a 51% answer lead to different actions, even when both pick the same option.

      
Skill
51.90 · 61.78
Raw accuracy flatters easy tests: guessing already gets 50% on a yes/no question, but under 2% with 59 choices. Skill removes what guessing would earn, so 0 means no better than guessing and 100 means perfect. 75% on a yes/no test is a skill of 50.

      
ECE
3.9 · 5.6
Take 100 answers the model called 80% likely. If 80 were right, the model was honest, and the gap is zero. ECE is that gap averaged across every confidence level, in percentage points.

      
NLL
0.87 · 0.72
A penalty based on the probability the model gave the right answer. Lower is better, and being sure and wrong is punished hardest.
0.05right answer given 95%
0.69right answer given 50%
4.61right answer given just 1%

      
Confident and wrong
0.9% · 1.7%
The share of answers that were at least 95% sure and still wrong. These are the errors that get past an automatic threshold. For comparison: GLiDE 27B 0.9%, mammoth-1 1.4%, Perplexity Decider v1.2 1.8%, Jev 1.14 2.7%.

      
Latency
30 · 43 ms
Half of all text requests finish within this time, measured with the vLLM inference engine. A decision usually runs inside another step, like routing a ticket, so this time adds directly to what the user waits for.

Show the numbers

    
Does the order of the options change the answer?

    
A decision shouldn’t depend on which option was listed first. We put about 2,000 public-suite questions through four shuffles of their options and counted the questions whose answer changed. Lower is better.

    
Turbo changed 3.7% of its answers and Flash 4.6%, roughly a third of Jev 1.14’s 12.3%, and clearly below GLiDE, mammoth and Perplexity Decider. Re-sending the same request never changed an answer. Sarvam Prism’s score with its options as sent is level with ours, and its fixed-order mode and Clef are lower still.

Indic languages

    
Indian languages, almost level with English

    
Most models are built English-first and treat other languages as a translation problem. Indian users don’t type that way. They switch languages mid-sentence, write Hindi in Roman letters and mix in English words.
Flash and Turbo are trained on that kind of input, so the same request works in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Telugu or Hinglish.

    
Someone says“Set an alarm for seven tomorrow morning.”
What “intent” means. Intent is what the person wants done. In this test the model reads one sentence and has to pick the right request from 59 possibilities, such as setting an alarm, playing music or reading the news. Guessing would be right less than 2% of the time.

    

    

  


  

    
Audio

    
They listen, in the languages people actually speak

    
The first challenge was that the 26B model behind Turbo has no audio input at all. We solved that by adding an audio tower to Gemma 26B and training it for decision work. Flash’s base hears natively.
From there, both models were trained to handle many kinds of listening work: what was said, how it was said, what else can be heard, and who is talking. Each score below is from held-out test data the models did not train on.

Text and tools

    
Fit for agents: when to call a tool, and what to distrust

    
An AI agent is software that gets things done for you: it checks the weather, books the cab, sends the email. Each of those actions is a tool. Before every step, the agent has to decide which tool fits the request, or whether it should use none at all.

    

    

      
The choices look alike
Real agents carry long lists of tools with similar names and overlapping jobs.

      
Sometimes the answer is no tool
A vague or off-topic request should stop the agent, and that’s harder than picking one.

      
A wrong call does something real
A bad sentence can be ignored. A bad tool call sends the message or moves the money.

    

    
Why it matters so much for a decision model. This choice comes up at every step of every agent run, so it has to be fast, and it needs a confidence you can trust before anything irreversible happens. That is exactly what a decision model is built for. It’s also the family’s strongest area on the public index.

Which agent caused the failure?

    
Agents fail in long, messy logs, and someone has to work out where it went wrong. We tested both models on Who&When, a public set of 184 failed multi-agent runs labelled with the agent responsible. Neither model trained on it, and it’s a task they weren’t tuned for.
