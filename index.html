The name

A word India already shares

Nirnaya is the Hindi word for a decision. It comes from Sanskrit, from roots that mean “to lead out”: you weigh what’s in front of you and arrive at a conclusion. Forms of the word live on in languages across India.

That shaped what we built. People make decisions in many languages, and they don’t only type them: they speak, and they point at photos and documents. So every Nirnaya model reads text, images and audio, and treats Indian languages as core work.

What a decision model is

Most decisions don’t need a conversation

Daniel Kahneman split human thinking into two modes. System 1 is fast and automatic: you read a face or hear that a voice is angry before you’ve tried to. System 2 is slow and deliberate: it’s what you use for long division.

Software is full of System 1 work. Which team should get this ticket? Is this message fraud? Should the agent call a tool here? Is this answer backed by the document? Each one is a choice between known options, and a product may make millions of them a day.

A decision model is built for exactly that. You give it a state and typed questions. It returns one answer per question, with a probability on every option, in a single pass. There is no text to generate and no reply to parse.

Why it exists

    
Why a normal LLM isn’t enough

    
An LLM can answer all of these questions. Running it in production as a decision engine is where the trouble starts.

    

      
It writes when you only need a choice
An LLM produces its answer one token at a time, and often explains itself first. You pay for those tokens in time and money. Nirnaya reads the request once and scores every option at once.

      
Its confidence isn’t measured
“I’m 90% sure” is a sentence, and nothing makes it true. A decision model’s probabilities are trained and checked against outcomes, so you can set a threshold on them.

      
Its answers need parsing
Free text can drift, add a preamble or name an option you didn’t offer. Here the response is always one of your options, with a probability for each.

      
Many options get expensive
Nirnaya handles up to 255 options per question and several questions in one request, still in one pass.

    

    
Training is what makes the difference. Here are Turbo’s base weights before and after fine-tuning, on 9,303 questions the model never trained on:

    

      
Accuracy48.6% → 83.4%

      
Calibration error (ECE, lower is better)23.5 → 3.5

      
Log loss (NLL, lower is better)1.83 → 0.52

    

    
“Before” uses the base weights with the final decision head. That head was trained alongside the fine-tuned weights, so this comparison is harder on “before” than a purpose-built baseline would be.

    
LLMs still do the jobs decisions can’t: explaining, drafting and reasoning through something new. The two work well together. Nirnaya handles the confident majority and hands the unsure cases to an LLM or a person.

What to measure

    
A good decision model is right, honest and fast

    

      
Probability
every option
The output itself. A 94% answer and a 51% answer lead to different actions, even when both pick the same option.

      
Skill
52.63 · 61.78
Accuracy corrected for chance, so a yes/no test and a 59-way test can share a scale. 0 is random guessing and 100 is perfect.

      
ECE
2.7 · 5.6
Expected calibration error: the gap between stated confidence and actual accuracy. Lower is better.

      
NLL
0.84 · 0.72
Negative log-likelihood. It rewards high probability on the right answer and punishes confident mistakes heavily. Lower is better.

      
Confident and wrong
0.8% · 1.7%
The share of answers that were at least 95% sure and still wrong. These are the errors that get past an automatic threshold.

      
Latency
31 · 43 ms
Median per request. The board’s limit is 1,000 ms, because a slow reflex isn’t a reflex.

Indic languages

    
Indian languages, almost level with English

    
On the same 59-way intent test, Turbo scores within 4 points of English in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali and Telugu, and Flash within about 6. On code-mixed Hinglish both reach 90%, across 16 options. Hindi general knowledge is harder, and Turbo’s lead shows there.

Audio

    
They listen, in the languages people actually speak

    
Both models match spoken sentences in six languages at or near 100%, work out a sentence’s topic and handle voice commands, directly from sound. The stock 26B model has no audio input, so we added an audio tower to Turbo and trained it. Flash, whose base has audio built in, leads on most sound and emotion tests.

    
Near-perfect scores should raise suspicion, so we reran Turbo on these tests with the audio replaced by silence. Spoken-sentence accuracy fell to about chance, which shows those answers come from listening. Voice commands stay well above chance without sound, so read that score with more care.

Images

    
Documents, charts and everyday Indian scenes

    
This is where Turbo’s extra size pays off. It scores 72.51 on the public vision index, against 59.63 for Flash, with 92.9 on infographics, 92.2 on key information from receipts and forms, and 80.2 on charts. It also holds up on images with an Indian cultural context, with questions asked in Indian languages.

Text and tools

    
Fit for agents: when to call a tool, and what to distrust

    
Tools are the family’s strongest public area, at 81.1 for Turbo and 74.4 for Flash. Both score 97.2 on function-calling selection (BFCL). On intent detection with out-of-scope requests (CLINC150+OOS), Turbo scores 90.0 and Flash 87.9. Several yes/no decisions fit in one pass, so one call can settle a whole tool list.

    
Which agent caused the failure?

    
Agents fail in long, messy logs, and someone has to work out where it went wrong. We tested both models on Who&When, a public set of 184 failed multi-agent runs labelled with the agent responsible. Neither model trained on it, and it’s a task they weren’t tuned for.

    
Accuracy on the 153 we could scoreCounting the other 31 as wrongWhen at least 70% sure
Flash52.9%44.0%22 of 35 right
Turbo62.7%52.2%54 of 65 right

    
Guessing would score 26.6% on those 153. With so few runs, each score carries a margin of about ±8 points. The dataset’s paper reports 53.5% for its best method on all 184 runs, using a different setup, so that isn’t a direct comparison. Of the 31 unscored runs, 11 were too long for the model’s window and 20 had labels we couldn’t match to the log or only one agent.

How we measured

    
Check the numbers yourself

    

      
Public text and vision indexes. Complete runs of the open Decision Index kit: 140,178 text requests and 12,210 vision rows per model, on NVIDIA H200 GPUs, so latency on other hardware will differ. The board re-scores submissions and runs its own private tests, and those scores are the ones that rank models.

      
Calibration. ECE, NLL and the confident-wrong share are our calculation with the board’s published definition (equal weight per benchmark, choice questions). The board publishes its own figures after review.

      
Language, audio and image tests. 32 held-out tests of up to 300 questions each, so each score carries a margin of about ±4 to 5 points, and small gaps between the models may be noise. An exact-match check found no question or media file shared with either model’s 1,076,823 training rows. Accuracy is choosing among the listed options, which isn’t the same as open transcription or free-form answering.
